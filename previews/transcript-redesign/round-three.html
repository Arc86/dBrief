UWV presentation preparation

24 September 202648m 16s5 actions3 tags

Summary

The team reviewed how to prepare the UWV presentation so that the audience can follow the reasoning, understand the recommendation, and see what happens next. The main agreement was to lead with the current situation rather than begin with a product overview. Option B will provide the central narrative, while the other options remain available in the appendix. The presentation should feel like a clear response to the meeting’s questions, with enough supporting material for a useful discussion.

Preparation will continue in parallel: Jesper will review the message and provide feedback on the draft, Stella will gather input from the UWV team and confirm the stakeholder review date, and the draft deck will provide the shared starting point. The team wants the next review to focus on the quality of the argument and the decisions still needed, rather than spend most of the session reconstructing the context.

Start with the audience’s situation

The opening should establish what the audience is trying to achieve and why the current approach deserves attention. Participants discussed the risk of moving too quickly into the recommendation before everyone agrees on the problem. A short account of the present situation will help the audience connect the proposal to work they recognise. It should explain the practical constraints and the questions raised during the preparation meeting, using language that makes sense without specialist knowledge.

The team also agreed that the introduction should stay selective. It does not need to reproduce every point from the conversation. Instead, it should identify the few facts that make the recommendation understandable. Supporting details can be retained for questions, while the first slides create a shared basis for evaluating the options. This keeps the deck useful for attendees who join with different levels of background knowledge.

Make Option B the central recommendation

Option B was preferred because it gives the presentation a clearer sequence: current situation, proposed approach, evidence, and next steps. The recommendation needs to state what the team is proposing and connect it to the situation described at the start. The presentation should make the reasoning visible rather than ask the audience to accept a conclusion without context. Participants want the transition from problem to proposal to feel deliberate and easy to follow.

The alternative options remain relevant, but giving each of them equal space in the main narrative would make the deck harder to read. Keeping them in the appendix will let the presenter answer questions and explain trade-offs without interrupting the recommended path. Their presence should support the discussion, particularly when a stakeholder asks why another approach was not selected, while preserving one clear recommendation in the core deck.

Support the argument with evidence

The evidence should help the audience assess the proposal, not simply make the deck appear more complete. The team discussed connecting each supporting point to a specific part of the recommendation. Material that does not change the audience’s understanding or help answer a likely question can move to the appendix. The first draft will be an opportunity to check whether the narrative remains understandable when the presenter is not there to explain every slide.

Jesper’s message review will focus on that connection between claim and support. Stella’s input from the UWV team will help clarify which details need to be addressed before the stakeholder review. The team should distinguish between information already available and points that still need confirmation. Open questions can be stated plainly rather than hidden inside a conclusion that sounds more certain than the meeting supports.

Use the first draft to resolve the remaining questions

The draft deck will bring the discussion into a concrete form. Participants want to review the order of the slides, the wording of the core message, and the amount of detail in the main narrative. The draft should make it possible to see where the argument is strong and where it depends on input that is still missing. It is a working document for the next discussion, not a signal that all decisions are complete.

Feedback should be specific enough for the draft owner to act on. If a slide is unclear, the review should identify whether the issue is the message, its supporting evidence, or its place in the sequence. If a point belongs in the appendix, the reason should be stated. This will help avoid a review that produces several competing edits without explaining which version best serves the audience.

Coordinate preparation across the team

Jesper will review the presentation’s core message and send feedback on the first draft. Stella will collect the remaining UWV input and confirm the stakeholder review date. The preparation work depends on these contributions arriving in a form that can be incorporated into the deck. The team should use the draft as the shared reference so that comments refer to the same narrative and do not create separate versions of the recommendation.

Timing remains a point to confirm. The meeting established the need for a follow-up review, but preparation still requires coordination with the people providing input. The next review should make room for the unresolved questions about stakeholder feedback and the presentation sequence. Where an answer is not yet available, the deck can preserve the question without implying that it has already been settled.

What the next review needs to establish

The next review should confirm that the opening describes the current situation clearly, the recommendation follows from that context, and the supporting material is sufficient for the intended discussion. It should also check that the alternatives are available in the appendix and that the final section explains the next steps. The team wants the audience to leave with an understanding of both the proposal and the work that follows it.
